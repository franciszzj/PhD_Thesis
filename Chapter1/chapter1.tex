\chapter{Introduction}

\section{Motivation}
Visual intelligence is increasingly defined by two complementary capabilities: \emph{visual understanding} and \emph{visual generation}.
Visual understanding converts pixels into structured, queryable knowledge about entities and their interactions, while visual generation creates visual content that is not only realistic, but also faithful to explicit intent, enabling interactive creation and editing.

This duality closely mirrors human vision.
Human vision is not a passive classifier; it is a compositional reasoning engine.
When observing a scene, humans effortlessly decompose it into objects, localize them in space, infer attributes, and reason about relations---who holds what, what occludes what, and which parts belong to which instances.
Equally importantly, humans can imagine with intent: they can mentally render an object from a description, edit a scene while preserving identity, or generate plausible variations that satisfy constraints. These abilities highlight that visual intelligence fundamentally involves structured understanding and controllable synthesis, rather than isolated recognition or unconstrained generation.

Recent years have witnessed rapid progress toward these goals. Transformer-based architectures~\citep{vaswani2017attention} have reshaped recognition and dense prediction, enabling end-to-end object discovery and set prediction~\citep{carion2020end} as well as strong mask-centric segmentation and panoptic parsing~\citep{cheng2022masked}. In parallel, vision--language pretraining has connected visual representations with natural language supervision~\citep{radford2021learning}, improving task transfer and enabling open-vocabulary recognition. On the generation side, diffusion models~\citep{ho2020denoising, rombach2022high} have pushed image and video synthesis toward photorealism and strong promptability. Yet, despite these advances, practical systems still struggle in scenarios that demand compositional structure, relational reasoning, or precise control---capabilities that come naturally to human visual perception and imagination.

Two gaps are especially persistent. First, many recognition systems output \emph{labels} rather than \emph{structured explanations}. A detector may list objects but omit the relations that make the scene meaningful; a segmenter may delineate regions but not articulate why a region matters or how it interacts with others. Second, many generation systems remain \emph{under-specified} or \emph{under-constrained}. Text-only prompting is expressive but ambiguous; it often lacks the geometric and identity constraints that users implicitly assume. As a result, models may generate plausible imagery while failing to preserve key invariants (identity, logos, background) or to satisfy a set of explicit conditions consistently.

This thesis takes the view that these gaps are not merely engineering issues, but reflect missing algorithmic ingredients. Structured understanding needs representations that are grounded, queryable, and compositional. Controllable generation needs interfaces that make constraints explicit and mechanisms that reliably bind those constraints to the correct spatial and temporal regions. A unifying theme across both sides is \emph{alignment}: aligning predicted elements to the right supervision signals during training (e.g., set prediction matching, debiased learning), and aligning conditioning signals to the right content during inference (e.g., attention-based correspondence for reference control).

On the understanding side, predictions are often \emph{insufficiently structured}. Models may identify objects but fail to produce coherent relational descriptions that support reasoning, especially when predicates are long-tailed or when the vocabulary shifts beyond the closed-set training labels.
On the generation side, outputs are often \emph{insufficiently controllable}. Even strong generators may partially follow instructions, entangle multiple constraints, over-edit irrelevant regions, or drift over time in video. These gaps motivate the central focus of this thesis.

This thesis, \emph{Visual Intelligence: Structured Understanding and Controllable Generation}, explores one representative direction for each capability. For visual understanding, we study \emph{panoptic scene graph generation} (PSG)~\citep{yang2022panoptic}, a task that forces models to jointly solve dense recognition and relational reasoning under realistic data bias and long-tail distributions. For visual generation, we study \emph{controllable image and video generation} with diffusion models~\citep{ho2020denoising, rombach2022high}, where synthesis must faithfully satisfy explicit constraints over content, layout, identity, and motion.

\section{Key Challenges and Research Questions}
The breadth of ``visual intelligence'' can be overwhelming. This thesis focuses on a set of recurring technical challenges that arise across tasks and that are central to building dependable systems.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{How should a scene be represented?} A good representation should be grounded in the image, support queries and reasoning, and remain scalable. Pixel-level outputs provide strong geometric grounding, while graph structure provides compositionality. PSG combines these properties by using masks for nodes and relations for edges.
    \item \textbf{How can models learn under long-tailed, biased supervision?} Relation labels are notoriously imbalanced and entangled with context. Models may achieve high average accuracy by exploiting shortcuts but fail on rare, semantically important cases. We study learning strategies and architectures that explicitly confront semantic overlap and frequency bias.
    \item \textbf{How can systems generalize beyond closed vocabularies?} Real-world scenes contain novel objects and relations. This motivates open-vocabulary and open-set learning, where language provides a flexible interface for expanding the label space. We study how prompting and multimodal models can improve generalization while maintaining grounding.
    \item \textbf{How can generation become reliably controllable?} Diffusion models are powerful, but controllability requires more than realism. The generator must localize edits, preserve identity, and satisfy multiple constraints without interference. Reference-based control highlights the correspondence problem: transferring fine details to the correct regions.
    \item \textbf{How can controllability scale to video?} Video generation adds long-range temporal dependencies. Identity and structure must remain coherent over time, and the data required for reference-conditioned training can be expensive to curate. We study scalable training paradigms that simulate reference conditioning and reduce reliance on specialized datasets.
\end{itemize}
The remainder of this introduction positions the thesis contributions within these questions.
Across both themes, we repeatedly ask: \emph{what is the right unit of prediction} (masks, pairs, phrases, frames) and \emph{how can we align these units with supervision and control signals}? In PSG, alignment appears as set prediction and matching (which prediction corresponds to which ground-truth entity) and as debiased learning (which cues actually support a predicate). In controllable diffusion, alignment appears as attention-based correspondence (which reference region supports which generated region) and as temporally stable conditioning (which identity features persist across frames). These parallels are intentional: they provide a consistent conceptual lens for interpreting the diverse methods in later chapters.

\section{Visual Understanding via Panoptic Scene Graph Generation}
Panoptic scene graphs offer a unified, holistic representation of a scene. Nodes correspond to panoptic entities---pixel-accurate ``things'' instances and ``stuff'' regions---and edges represent semantic relations between entity pairs. Compared with object-only graphs or box-based scene graphs, PSG is grounded at the pixel level and describes the full image, making it better aligned with downstream reasoning tasks where context and relations matter.

PSG sits at the intersection of panoptic segmentation~\citep{kirillov2019panoptic} and scene graph generation~\citep{lu2016visual}. It inherits the pixel-level grounding and the requirement to cover the entire image from panoptic segmentation, while inheriting the need for compositional relational reasoning from scene graphs. This combination makes PSG attractive as an intermediate representation: the resulting graph is explicit, queryable, and suitable for downstream tasks such as captioning, retrieval, and reasoning~\citep{hildebrandt2020scene, chen2020say, shi2019explainable}.

PSG is challenging because it couples multiple hard subproblems~\citep{yang2022panoptic}. It requires \textbf{cross-granularity modeling}: relations must be inferred from both fine localization cues (e.g., boundaries, overlaps, contact patterns) and coarse semantics (e.g., functional interactions). It exhibits \textbf{severe long-tail imbalance}: a small number of common predicates dominate, while rare relations are often the most informative; this echoes long-standing dataset bias and imbalance issues in visual relationship detection and scene graphs~\citep{zellers2018neural, cheng2022visual}. It also calls for \textbf{open-world generalization}: real scenes contain novel categories and relations, and a practical PSG system must scale beyond fixed label sets, aligning with the broader trend of open-vocabulary recognition driven by vision--language models~\citep{radford2021learning, wu2024towards}. Chapters~3--5 address these challenges through successive frameworks that (i) improve structure modeling under long-tail and semantic overlap, (ii) incorporate language priors via prompting, and (iii) extend PSG to open-set relation prediction with large multimodal models.

Conceptually, PSG asks for ``what is in the image'' and ``how are those things related'' in a single output. Algorithmically, this means that errors compound: if an entity mask is missed or mislabeled, relations involving that entity cannot be predicted correctly; if relation prediction is biased toward frequent predicates, the resulting graph becomes uninformative even when the entities are correct. PSG therefore benefits from end-to-end designs that couple segmentation and relation reasoning, but it also highlights the tension between objectives: models must allocate capacity to precise localization \emph{and} to relational semantics.

From the perspective of learning, PSG surfaces two forms of ambiguity that are often hidden in simpler tasks. First, predicates can be multi-granular: a person and a chair may be annotated as \emph{sitting on}, while \emph{on} is also correct at a coarser level. Second, predicates can be context-sensitive: the same pair of entities can have different relations depending on surrounding objects and the global scene. These properties motivate designs that explicitly represent multiple semantic granularities and that incorporate contextual priors without collapsing to frequency shortcuts.

Finally, PSG provides a natural bridge to open-world reasoning. Since relations are linguistic constructs, language models can supply priors about which relations are plausible, how predicates relate to each other, and how to paraphrase a relation when the exact label is unavailable. However, using language priors responsibly requires grounding: the model must still justify a relation with visual evidence. Chapters~4--5 explore this balance by injecting language features into relation decoding and by moving toward phrase-based, open-set relation prediction with multimodal models.

An additional practical consideration is \textbf{evaluation}. PSG sits on top of multiple components, and improvements in one component can mask regressions in another. For example, stronger segmentation improves entity grounding but may reduce the number of candidate entities, affecting relation recall; conversely, aggressive relation prediction can inflate recall while harming precision and interpretability. PSG benchmarks therefore often report both segmentation metrics (e.g., panoptic quality) and relation metrics (e.g., Recall and mean Recall) to disentangle where gains come from. Throughout Chapters~3--5, we emphasize analyses that isolate long-tail behavior, semantic overlap effects, and open-set generalization, since these are the regimes where PSG systems are most likely to fail in realistic usage.

\section{Visual Generation via Controllable Image and Video Synthesis}
Generative models have become a powerful interface for content creation. However, many real applications demand fine-grained control: users specify \emph{what} should appear, \emph{where} it should appear, \emph{how} it should look, and \emph{what should remain unchanged}. Such requirements arise in editing, layout-to-image synthesis, identity-preserving generation, and multimodal conditioning scenarios. In these settings, the key challenge is not producing a plausible sample, but producing an output that satisfies explicit constraints while maintaining realism.

Modern controllable generation is largely built on diffusion models~\citep{ho2020denoising, rombach2022high} because their iterative denoising process provides multiple injection points for conditioning signals and editing constraints. Practical control interfaces span text prompts, reference images, and explicit structure (e.g., poses, layouts, segmentation, and depth). A recurring failure mode is a \textbf{control--quality trade-off}: strong conditioning may harm realism, while weak conditioning yields high quality but fails to follow constraints. Explicit mechanisms such as additional control branches (e.g., ControlNet~\citep{zhang2023adding}) and parameter-efficient adaptation are often used to improve faithfulness without retraining the full model.

Video further elevates the difficulty. Control must be maintained not only spatially but also temporally: identity, texture, and structure should remain consistent across frames; motion should be coherent; and edits should not introduce flicker or drift. The recent wave of diffusion-based video generation extends text-to-image backbones with temporal modules or transformer-based video diffusion at scale~\citep{blattmann2023stable, guo2023animatediff, peebles2023scalable}. Yet, personalized video generation conditioned on reference images remains particularly challenging due to data construction and long-range consistency issues. Chapters~6--7 study controllable image and video generation and develop mechanisms that improve faithfulness under complex, multi-condition constraints.

From an algorithmic standpoint, controllable generation can be viewed as learning a conditional distribution $p(x \mid c)$ where the condition $c$ is rich and multi-modal. Different conditions play different roles. Text provides high-level semantics; geometric signals such as pose and segmentation constrain spatial structure; reference images specify appearance and identity. Combining these signals is difficult because they can conflict. For example, a text prompt may describe a garment that differs from the reference clothing, or a pose condition may require large deformations that make detail transfer challenging. Reliable systems must therefore prioritize constraints and resolve conflicts without degrading quality.

Reference-conditioned generation exposes a particularly sharp version of this problem: \textbf{correspondence}. Fine-grained details such as logos and textures must be mapped from the reference to the correct location in the output under potentially large pose and viewpoint changes. Many failures of controllable diffusion (blurry text, distorted patterns, identity drift) can be traced to misaligned correspondence during denoising. Chapter~6 addresses this by regularizing attention-based correspondence during training, making reference transfer more faithful without adding inference cost.

For video, correspondence becomes spatiotemporal: identity must be consistent across time and also consistent with the reference. Moreover, collecting triplet supervision (reference, video, text) is expensive, which can limit scale and diversity. Chapter~7 therefore explores a scalable alternative: training on video--text pairs while \emph{simulating} reference conditioning through masking strategies and attention masks. This reframes identity-preserving control as a form of masked reconstruction, encouraging the model to learn stable identity features that persist over time.

Just as in PSG, \textbf{evaluation} is subtle. Traditional realism metrics (e.g., FID for images) do not measure whether the output satisfies constraints. In controllable generation, the most important failures are often local and semantic: a logo is distorted, a reference face changes slightly, or an edit bleeds into the background. For video, small per-frame deviations can accumulate into noticeable drift. Chapters~6--7 therefore emphasize controllability-focused evaluation, including identity preservation and temporal consistency, and use stress tests that amplify common failure modes (large pose changes, multi-reference inputs, longer temporal horizons). This evaluation perspective also motivates the thesis focus on lightweight, training-time mechanisms: to be practical, controllability improvements should generalize across backbones and should not impose heavy inference overhead.

\section{Overview of Contributions}
The thesis comprises five papers, organized into two themes.

Across these papers, two methodological motifs recur. On the understanding side, we repeatedly confront dataset bias and semantic ambiguity by making structure explicit (multi-granular relation modeling, efficiency-aware pair reasoning) and by leveraging language as a source of priors. On the generation side, we treat control as an explicit objective and focus on correspondence mechanisms that bind conditioning signals (references, masks) to the correct spatial/temporal regions during denoising.

\subsection{Panoptic Scene Graph Generation (Chapters~3--5)}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{HiLo (Chapter~3)}~\citep{zhou2023hilo} identifies a key gap in long-tail PSG: predicates are not only imbalanced, but also semantically overlapping, so a single pair of entities may admit multiple plausible relations at different semantic granularities. HiLo introduces frequency-specialized branches for high- and low-frequency predicates and enforces cross-branch consistency, enabling models to recover informative rare relations while preserving strong overall recall. We also develop an efficient one-stage PSG baseline that tightly couples mask prediction and relation decoding.
    \item \textbf{VLPrompt (Chapter~4)}~\citep{zhou2023vlprompt} brings language into fully supervised PSG by using large language models to elicit context-rich relation descriptions. A dual-attention prompter fuses object-pair visual features with complementary language signals (proposal and judgement prompts), and a gating mechanism adaptively combines them. This improves rare predicate prediction and generalization, while keeping the interface lightweight and transferable.
    \item \textbf{OpenPSG (Chapter~5)}~\citep{zhou2025openpsg} formulates and tackles open-set PSG, combining an open-set panoptic segmenter with an efficient relation query transformer and an autoregressive multimodal relation decoder. The model predicts relation phrases beyond predefined vocabularies and uses instruction design to improve grounded relation generation, pushing PSG toward scalable open-world usage.
\end{itemize}
\noindent These three chapters can be read as a progression from \emph{robust closed-set prediction} (handle long-tail and semantic overlap) to \emph{language-augmented prediction} (use prompts to inject priors) and finally to \emph{open-set prediction} (generate relation phrases beyond a fixed predicate list). Each step introduces additional flexibility, but also additional algorithmic requirements: calibration, efficiency, and grounding.

\subsection{Controllable Image and Video Generation (Chapters~6--7)}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Leffa (Chapter~6)}~\citep{zhou2025learning} targets reference-based controllable person image generation, where models must preserve fine-grained details from reference images while satisfying pose or clothing constraints. We propose a training-time regularization that guides cross-reference attention through learnable flow fields, improving correspondence and detail preservation without additional parameters or inference cost, and generalizing across diffusion backbones and tasks (virtual try-on and pose transfer).
    \item \textbf{Saber (Chapter~7)}~\citep{zhou2025scaling} addresses reference-to-video generation at scale. Instead of relying on expensive reference image--video--text triplets, Saber learns reference-aware identity consistency through masked training on video--text pairs, complemented by attention masking and spatial mask augmentations that mitigate copy--paste artifacts. This yields a scalable, zero-shot framework that generalizes across a varying number of reference images and improves temporal coherence.
\end{itemize}
\noindent Together, Chapters~6--7 emphasize that controllability is best treated as an \emph{alignment} problem: the model must align generated content with the correct reference regions (spatial alignment) and keep that alignment stable over time (temporal alignment). The contributions therefore focus on training objectives and masking strategies that make correspondence learnable and reliable, rather than relying on expensive inference-time modules.

\section{Thesis Organization}
Chapter~2 reviews related work on visual understanding and generation, with emphasis on structured representations (panoptic segmentation, scene graphs, and PSG), long-tail and open-world learning, vision--language prompting and large multimodal models, as well as diffusion-based conditioning and temporal consistency mechanisms for controllable video generation.
Chapters~3--5 present the structured understanding contributions and progressively broaden PSG capability from long-tail robustness (Chapter~3) to vision--language prompting (Chapter~4) and open-set relation prediction (Chapter~5).
Chapters~6--7 present the controllable generation contributions, focusing on attention-based correspondence for reference-guided person image generation (Chapter~6) and scalable zero-shot reference-to-video generation trained without explicit triplet data (Chapter~7).
Chapter~8 concludes with a summary, limitations, and future research directions that connect structured understanding with structure-aware controllable generation.

For readers primarily interested in \emph{structured understanding}, Chapters~3--5 provide a unified view of PSG systems: how to model relations under long-tail bias, how to incorporate language priors without sacrificing grounding, and how to move toward open-world settings where predicate vocabularies are not fixed. For readers primarily interested in \emph{controllable generation}, Chapters~6--7 focus on correspondence and identity preservation in diffusion models, from single-image person generation to scalable reference-to-video generation. Chapter~2 provides algorithmic background (attention, set prediction, diffusion, control interfaces) to make these chapters self-contained.
When possible, each chapter states its assumptions, problem setting, and evaluation protocols explicitly, so that readers can interpret the contributions even without deep familiarity with the surrounding literature. We also aim to connect the two themes by highlighting shared primitives (attention, matching, alignment) that recur across understanding and generation.
