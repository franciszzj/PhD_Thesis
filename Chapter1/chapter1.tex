\chapter{Introduction}

\section{Motivation}
Visual intelligence is increasingly defined by two complementary capabilities: \emph{visual understanding} and \emph{visual generation}. Visual understanding converts pixels into structured, queryable knowledge about entities and their interactions, while visual generation creates visual content that is not only realistic, but also faithful to explicit intent, enabling interactive creation and editing.

This duality closely mirrors human vision. Human vision is not a passive classifier; it is a compositional reasoning engine. When observing a scene, humans effortlessly decompose it into objects, localize them in space, infer attributes, and reason about relations—who holds what, what occludes what, and which parts belong to which instances. Equally importantly, humans can imagine with intent: they can mentally render an object from a description, edit a scene while preserving identity, or generate plausible variations that satisfy constraints. These abilities highlight that visual intelligence fundamentally involves structured understanding and controllable synthesis, rather than isolated recognition or unconstrained generation.

Recent years have witnessed rapid progress toward these goals. Transformer-based architectures and dense prediction models have significantly improved segmentation and recognition; vision–language pretraining has enabled transfer across tasks and open vocabularies; and diffusion models have pushed visual synthesis to photorealistic levels. Yet, despite these advances, practical systems still struggle in scenarios that demand compositional structure, relational reasoning, or precise control—capabilities that come naturally to human visual perception and imagination.

On the understanding side, predictions are often \emph{insufficiently structured}. Models may identify objects but fail to produce coherent relational descriptions that support reasoning, especially when predicates are long-tailed or when the vocabulary shifts beyond the closed-set training labels.
On the generation side, outputs are often \emph{insufficiently controllable}. Even strong generators may partially follow instructions, entangle multiple constraints, over-edit irrelevant regions, or drift over time in video. These gaps motivate the central focus of this thesis.

This thesis, \emph{Visual Intelligence: Structured Understanding and Controllable Generation}, explores one representative direction for each capability. For visual understanding, we study \emph{panoptic scene graph generation} (PSG). For visual generation, we study \emph{controllable image and video generation}.

\section{Visual Understanding via Panoptic Scene Graph Generation}
Panoptic scene graphs offer a unified, holistic representation of a scene. Nodes correspond to panoptic entities---pixel-accurate ``things'' instances and ``stuff'' regions---and edges represent semantic relations between entity pairs. Compared with object-only graphs or box-based scene graphs, PSG is grounded at the pixel level and describes the full image, making it better aligned with downstream reasoning tasks where context and relations matter.

PSG is challenging because it couples multiple hard subproblems. It requires \textbf{cross-granularity modeling}: relations must be inferred from both fine localization cues (e.g., boundaries, overlaps, contact patterns) and coarse semantics (e.g., functional interactions). It exhibits \textbf{severe long-tail imbalance}: a small number of common predicates dominate, while rare relations are often the most informative. It also calls for \textbf{open-world generalization}: real scenes contain novel categories and relations, and a practical PSG system must scale beyond fixed label sets. Chapters~3--5 address these challenges through successive frameworks that progressively improve structure modeling, incorporate language priors, and extend to open-vocabulary settings.

\section{Visual Generation via Controllable Image and Video Synthesis}
Generative models have become a powerful interface for content creation. However, many real applications demand fine-grained control: users specify \emph{what} should appear, \emph{where} it should appear, \emph{how} it should look, and \emph{what should remain unchanged}. Such requirements arise in editing, layout-to-image synthesis, identity-preserving generation, and multimodal conditioning scenarios. In these settings, the key challenge is not producing a plausible sample, but producing an output that satisfies explicit constraints while maintaining realism.

Video further elevates the difficulty. Control must be maintained not only spatially but also temporally: identity, texture, and structure should remain consistent across frames; motion should be coherent; and edits should not introduce flicker or drift. Chapters~6--7 study controllable image and video generation and develop mechanisms that improve faithfulness under complex, multi-condition constraints.

\section{Overview of Contributions}
The thesis comprises five papers, organized into two themes.

\subsection{Panoptic Scene Graph Generation (Chapters~3--5)}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{HiLo (Chapter~3)} studies PSG under long-tailed relations from a representation perspective, improving structure modeling across semantic granularity so that fine localization cues and high-level semantics are jointly exploited for relation prediction.
    \item \textbf{VLPrompt (Chapter~4)} introduces vision--language prompting for PSG as a lightweight and transferable mechanism that injects linguistic and commonsense priors, improving relation reasoning and generalization without relying solely on visual cues.
    \item \textbf{OpenPSG (Chapter~5)} pushes PSG toward open-vocabulary and open-set conditions, leveraging large multimodal models to scale relational reasoning beyond predefined label vocabularies and to better handle novel concepts.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Controllable Image and Video Generation (Chapters~6--7)}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Leffa (Chapter~6)} investigates control interfaces and intermediate representations that improve faithfulness for controllable image and video synthesis while preserving perceptual quality.
    \item \textbf{Saber (Chapter~7)} strengthens controllability under complex, multi-condition constraints and emphasizes robustness and temporal coherence for practical video generation and editing.
\end{itemize}

\section{Thesis Organization}
Chapter~2 reviews related work on structured visual understanding and controllable visual generation, with emphasis on PSG, vision--language prompting, open-vocabulary recognition, diffusion-based conditioning, and video consistency mechanisms.
Chapters~3--5 present the PSG contributions.
Chapters~6--7 present the controllable image and video generation contributions.
Chapter~8 concludes with a summary, limitations, and future research directions.
