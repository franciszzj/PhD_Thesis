\chapter{Introduction}

\section{Motivation}
This thesis studies \emph{control} in visual understanding and visual generation, that is, whether the output of a model follows the specification it is given. Both areas have progressed rapidly, and both remain weakly controllable for the same reason.

Understanding systems recognize objects but do not predict the relations between them, particularly under long-tailed and open-world distributions. Their output therefore reports what a scene contains rather than what occurs in it, and the relations they do report are drawn from a small set of frequent predicates. Generation systems synthesize realistic images and videos but violate fine-grained constraints on appearance, identity, and motion. A reference image conditions such a generator without constraining it, and the details that identify a subject are consequently lost. Neither limitation reflects insufficient model capacity. In both cases the required behavior is never expressed as a training target, so no gradient drives the model toward it.

This is not for want of capable architectures. Transformer-based architectures~\citep{vaswani2017attention} have reshaped recognition and dense prediction, enabling end-to-end object discovery and set prediction~\citep{carion2020end} as well as strong mask-centric segmentation and panoptic parsing~\citep{cheng2022masked}. In parallel, vision--language pretraining has connected visual representations with natural language supervision~\citep{radford2021learning}, improving task transfer and enabling open-vocabulary recognition. On the generation side, diffusion models~\citep{ho2020denoising, rombach2022high} have pushed image and video synthesis toward photorealism and strong promptability. None of these advances makes a model follow a specification, because none of them supervises that behavior.

The two gaps share a cause and therefore admit a common remedy. Understanding requires a representation that is pixel-grounded, compositional, and robust when supervision is biased or when the label space extends beyond a closed vocabulary. Generation requires a mechanism that determines which information to preserve from a reference and where to place it in space and time. In both settings the remedy takes the same form: the intended behavior is attached to an explicit quantity, and that quantity is supervised during training. A control signal that is merely provided as an input will not be used as intended.

Accordingly, this thesis develops two lines of work that share this remedy. For visual understanding, we study \emph{panoptic scene graph generation} (PSG)~\citep{yang2022panoptic}, which segments an image into panoptic entities and predicts subject--object--relation triplets to form a pixel-grounded scene graph over both ``things'' and ``stuff'' categories. For visual generation, we study \emph{reference-controlled image and video generation} with diffusion models~\citep{ho2020denoising, rombach2022high}, in which the output must remain faithful to user-specified appearance, identity, and motion constraints.

\section{A Unifying Argument: Control Must Be Learned, Not Offered}
\label{sec:unifying_argument}
These two lines of work are usually studied by separate communities, and they could easily be presented as unrelated. This thesis argues the opposite. Both concern the same property, which we call \emph{control}. A model is controllable if its output follows the specification it is given.

Two examples make the property concrete. A user of a scene understanding system usually needs more than a list of objects. Knowing that an image contains a person and a horse is far less useful than knowing that the person is \textit{riding} the horse, so the relations between objects are what the user actually wants to obtain. A user of a reference-conditioned generator has a request that is already explicit. The user supplies one or more reference images showing a particular garment, object, or person, and asks for an image or a video in which that subject appears exactly as given. In both cases the user has asked for something specific, and in both cases current systems fail to deliver it. The understanding system reports \textit{on} instead of \textit{riding}. The generator returns output that is plausible overall but does not preserve the identifying details, so a logo becomes illegible in a generated image, and a subject drifts from frame to frame in a generated video.

The important point is that neither failure is caused by missing information. The rare relation is in the vocabulary of the first model, and the subject that should have been preserved is in the reference images of the second. What is missing is any reason to use them, because nothing in the training objective makes the intended answer easier to produce than the convenient one. The central claim of this thesis follows:

\begin{quote}
\emph{Control has to be trained into a model rather than supplied to it. Giving a model a control signal does not make the model use that signal. The intended behavior must be attached to a concrete quantity and supervised directly, or the model will fall back on whatever the training data makes easiest.}
\end{quote}

\noindent The rest of this section develops this claim. We first describe what control means in each part of the thesis, then show that the same failure appears in all five technical chapters, and finally identify the mechanism that the two parts share.

\subsection*{What Control Means in Each Part}
In Part~I, control is applied \emph{to the scene representation}. A visual understanding system offers control to the extent that a user can specify what the system should report about a scene, and to the extent that the system honors that specification. By this measure a closed-set predicate classifier offers almost no control. Its output space is fixed during training, and its behavior is determined by the frequency distribution of the training predicates rather than by any user intent. Predicting relations between objects is what makes control possible in the first place, because a relation is the smallest unit of a scene description that a user can ask about, verify, or act on. Each of Chapters~3--5 then widens this interface. HiLo (Chapter~3) adds control over the head--tail trade-off, so that a model can recover rare but informative predicates without losing frequent ones. VLPrompt (Chapter~4) adds control through language, so that external knowledge can shape relation prediction while a gating network decides when to trust it. OpenPSG (Chapter~5) adds control over the vocabulary itself, so that the set of expressible relations is specified at inference time instead of being fixed in a classifier head.

In Part~II, control is applied \emph{to the generated output}, through pose, reference appearance, spatial correspondence, and temporal consistency. Here the user specification is explicit, since the user supplies a reference image and a prompt, and the question is whether the generator honors it. Leffa (Chapter~6) improves control over fine-grained appearance, so that details such as textures and logos are drawn from the correct reference regions. Saber (Chapter~7) improves control over identity across time, so that a subject specified by one or more references remains consistent throughout a generated video.

The two parts therefore study the same property in two places. Part~I asks whether a system can be controlled in what it reports about a scene. Part~II asks whether a system can be controlled in what it produces.

\subsection*{Why Control Has to Be Learned}
The examples above are not special cases. Every technical chapter in this thesis contains an instance of the same gap, in which the model is given what it needs and has no reason to use it.

In Chapter~3, the model is given a vocabulary of $56$ predicates, but the training objective gives it no reason to use the rare ones, so it predicts frequent predicates almost exclusively. In Chapter~4, the model is given rich language descriptions, but nothing forces it to weigh them against visual evidence, so it either ignores them or follows them uncritically. In Chapter~5, the model is given an open vocabulary through a multimodal decoder, but the decoder will produce a fluent relation for any pair it receives, whether or not a relation is present. In Chapter~6, the model is given a reference image, yet the same reference yields either faithful or distorted textures depending only on whether attention resolves correspondence correctly. In Chapter~7, the model is given reference frames, but it can satisfy the training objective by copying neighboring pixels instead of learning what identity to preserve.

In all five cases the information the model needs is available to it, and in all five cases the model does not use it. The reason is the same each time. Producing the intended answer is harder than producing the convenient one, and nothing in the objective compensates for that difference. This is why control must be trained. The objective has to make the intended behavior the easiest way to reduce the loss, instead of leaving it as one option among several.

\subsection*{Structure Is What Makes Control Trainable}
Teaching control requires something concrete to supervise, and this is where structure enters. Structure is not a second thesis-level claim. It is the means by which control becomes trainable, because it provides a quantity that a loss can be attached to.

In Part~I, that quantity is relational: subject--object--relation triplets grounded in panoptic entities. Because a triplet is explicit, its head and tail behavior can be separated (Chapter~3), a language prior can be attached to it (Chapter~4), and it can be generated rather than classified (Chapter~5). In Part~II, the quantity is geometric: the human pose that defines body configuration, the spatial correspondence that maps reference pixels to target pixels, and the temporal coherence that keeps a subject stable across frames. Because correspondence is explicit, it can be read out of the attention map and regularized directly (Chapter~6), and because the reference relation is explicit, it can be simulated through masking and supervised (Chapter~7).

In both parts the role of structure is the same. It converts a vague requirement, such as ``preserve the reference'' or ``do not ignore rare relations'', into a specific quantity that a training signal can act on. Where the structure is implicit, no such signal is available, and the model falls back on whatever the data makes easy.

\subsection*{The Shared Mechanism}
The two parts also share a single technical mechanism, which Section~\ref{sec:attention_soft_matching} develops in detail: \textbf{attention as soft matching}. Hungarian matching assigns predictions to ground-truth instances in set-based detection. Relation decoding assigns predicates to entity pairs. Cross-attention assigns target pixels to reference pixels during denoising. Temporal attention assigns tokens in one frame to tokens in another. All four are the same operation, a learned and differentiable assignment between two sets. This is why the two parts of this thesis have a common technical foundation rather than only a common topic.

The mechanism also explains the empirical pattern behind the central claim. When an assignment is left implicit, it collapses toward the statistically dominant answer, which is frequent predicates in Part~I and salient reference regions in Part~II. When the same assignment is supervised or regularized directly, the collapse does not occur. Control, in other words, is exactly the property that this assignment either has or lacks.

Chapter~8 revisits this claim and discusses where it holds, where it does not, and what it implies for future work.

\section{Key Challenges and Research Questions}
The claim above is broad, so this thesis concentrates on a fixed set of recurring technical questions that make it concrete.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Visual Understanding}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{How should a scene be represented so that it can be controlled?} The representation should be grounded in the image, support relational reasoning, and remain scalable. PSG combines these properties by using panoptic masks for entities and relations for edges~\citep{yang2022panoptic}.
        \item \textbf{How can relation prediction remain reliable under long-tail bias and semantic overlap?} Predicate labels are highly imbalanced, and multiple relations can be plausible for the same entity pair at different semantic granularities. We study architectures that explicitly address this ambiguity rather than collapsing to frequent shortcuts.
        \item \textbf{How can this control extend to open-world settings?} Real scenes contain novel objects and relations. This motivates methods that use language and multimodal models to expand beyond predefined relation vocabularies while preserving visual grounding.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Visual Generation}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{How can generation preserve the correct reference information?} Realistic synthesis is not enough; the model must preserve the right appearance and identity details and bind them to the correct spatial regions. This makes correspondence a central problem.
        \item \textbf{How can such controllability scale from images to videos?} Video adds long-range temporal dependencies, and reference-conditioned triplet data are expensive to collect. We therefore study training paradigms that learn reference-aware generation from more scalable supervision.
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
The remainder of this introduction places the contributions of this thesis within these questions. In PSG, the focus is on pixel-grounded entities, relation reasoning under biased supervision, and open-world generalization. In reference-controlled diffusion, the focus is on reference preservation, attention design, and temporal consistency. The two groups of questions are posed in different task settings, but they are two instances of the argument in Section~\ref{sec:unifying_argument}. Each asks how a control signal can be turned into behavior that the model actually follows.

\section{Control in Visual Understanding via Panoptic Scene Graph Generation}
Panoptic scene graph generation (PSG)~\citep{yang2022panoptic} is the structured-understanding setting studied in this thesis. PSG segments an image into panoptic entities and predicts subject--object--relation triplets, producing a pixel-grounded scene graph over both ``things'' and ``stuff'' categories. Compared with object-only graphs or box-based scene graphs, PSG grounds relations at the pixel level and describes the full image, making it a natural testbed for compositional reasoning.

PSG sits at the intersection of panoptic segmentation~\citep{kirillov2019panoptic} and scene graph generation~\citep{lu2016visual}. It inherits the pixel-level grounding and the requirement to cover the entire image from panoptic segmentation, while inheriting the need for compositional relational reasoning from scene graphs. This combination makes PSG attractive as an intermediate representation: the resulting graph is explicit, grounded, and suitable for downstream reasoning tasks such as captioning, retrieval, and reasoning~\citep{hildebrandt2020scene, chen2020say, shi2019explainable}.

PSG is challenging because it couples multiple hard subproblems~\citep{yang2022panoptic}. It requires \textbf{cross-granularity modeling}: relations must be inferred from both fine localization cues (\eg, boundaries, overlaps, contact patterns) and coarse semantics (\eg, functional interactions). It exhibits severe \textbf{long-tail imbalance}: a small number of common predicates dominate, while rare relations are often the most informative; this echoes long-standing dataset bias and imbalance issues in visual relationship detection and scene graphs~\citep{zellers2018neural, cheng2022visual}. It also calls for \textbf{open-world generalization}: real scenes contain novel categories and relations, and a practical PSG system must scale beyond fixed label sets. Chapters~3--5 address these challenges through successive frameworks that improve long-tail relation recognition under semantic overlap, introduce language-assisted relation prediction, and extend PSG to open-world settings with grounded phrase prediction.

PSG asks what is in the image and how those things are related, and answers both in a single output. Errors therefore compound. If an entity mask is missed or mislabeled, no relation involving that entity can be predicted correctly. If relation prediction is biased toward frequent predicates, the resulting graph is uninformative even when the entities are correct. PSG therefore benefits from end-to-end designs that couple segmentation and relation reasoning, but it also highlights the tension between objectives: models must allocate capacity to precise localization \emph{and} to relational semantics.

From the perspective of learning, PSG surfaces two forms of ambiguity that are often hidden in simpler tasks. First, predicates can be multi-granular: a person and a chair may be annotated as \emph{sitting on}, while \emph{on} is also correct at a coarser level. Second, predicates can be context-sensitive: the same pair of entities can have different relations depending on surrounding objects and the global scene. These properties motivate designs that explicitly represent multiple semantic granularities and that incorporate contextual priors without collapsing to frequency shortcuts~\citep{cheng2022visual, zhou2023hilo}.

Finally, PSG provides a natural bridge to open-world reasoning. Since relations are linguistic constructs, language models can supply priors about which relations are plausible, how predicates relate to each other, and how to paraphrase a relation when the exact label is unavailable~\citep{min2023recent, zhang2024vision}. However, using language priors responsibly requires grounding: the model must still justify a relation with visual evidence. Chapters~4--5 explore this balance by injecting language cues into relation decoding and by moving toward phrase-based, open-set relation prediction with multimodal models.

An additional practical consideration is \textbf{evaluation}. PSG sits on top of multiple components, and improvements in one component can mask regressions in another. For example, stronger segmentation improves entity grounding but may reduce the number of candidate entities, affecting relation recall; conversely, aggressive relation prediction can inflate recall while harming precision and interpretability. PSG benchmarks therefore often report both segmentation metrics (\eg, Panoptic Quality (PQ)) and relation metrics (\eg, Recall@$K$ and Mean Recall@$K$ (mR@$K$)) to disentangle where gains come from~\citep{kirillov2019panoptic, zellers2018neural, yang2022panoptic}. Throughout Chapters~3--5, we emphasize analyses that isolate long-tail behavior, semantic overlap effects, and open-set generalization, since these are the regimes where PSG systems are most likely to fail in realistic usage.

\section{Control in Visual Generation via Reference-Conditioned Synthesis}
The second direction of this thesis studies controllable image and video synthesis. The focus is not generic prompt following, but faithful generation under fine-grained constraints over appearance, identity, and motion. In the settings studied here, users provide reference images together with text or structural conditions, and the model must preserve the specified reference information while maintaining realism.

Modern controllable generation is largely built on diffusion models~\citep{ho2020denoising, rombach2022high} because their iterative denoising process provides multiple injection points for conditioning signals and editing constraints. Practical control interfaces span text prompts, reference images, and explicit structure (\eg, poses, layouts, segmentation, and depth). A recurring failure mode is a \textbf{control--quality trade-off}: strong conditioning may harm realism, while weak conditioning yields high quality but fails to follow constraints. Explicit mechanisms such as additional control branches (\eg, ControlNet~\citep{zhang2023adding}) and parameter-efficient adaptation are often used to improve faithfulness without retraining the full model.

Video further elevates the difficulty. Control must be maintained not only spatially but also temporally: identity, texture, and structure should remain consistent across frames; motion should be coherent; and edits should not introduce flicker or drift. The recent wave of diffusion-based video generation extends text-to-image backbones with temporal modules or transformer-based video diffusion at scale~\citep{blattmann2023stable, guo2023animatediff, peebles2023scalable}. Yet, reference-to-video generation remains particularly challenging because data with explicit reference supervision are expensive to construct and temporal consistency is hard to maintain. Chapters~6--7 therefore study how faithful controllability can be learned through training signals and attention design, rather than relying on expensive paired data or heavy inference-time machinery.

From an algorithmic standpoint, controllable generation can be viewed as learning a conditional distribution $p(x \mid c)$ where the condition $c$ is rich and multi-modal. Different conditions play different roles. Text provides high-level semantics; geometric signals such as pose and segmentation constrain spatial structure; reference images specify appearance and identity. Combining these signals is difficult because they can conflict. For example, a text prompt may describe a garment that differs from the reference clothing, or a pose condition may require large deformations that make detail transfer challenging. Reliable systems must therefore prioritize constraints and resolve conflicts without degrading quality.

Reference-conditioned generation exposes a particularly sharp version of this problem: \textbf{correspondence}. Fine-grained details such as logos and textures must be mapped from the reference to the correct location in the output under potentially large pose and viewpoint changes. Many failures of controllable diffusion (blurry text, distorted patterns, identity drift) can be traced to misaligned correspondence during denoising~\citep{chefer2023attend, cao2023masactrl, zhou2025learning}. Chapter~6 addresses this by regularizing attention-based correspondence during training, making reference transfer more faithful without adding inference cost.

For video, correspondence becomes spatiotemporal: identity must be consistent across time and also consistent with the reference. Moreover, collecting triplet supervision (reference, video, text) is expensive, which can limit scale and diversity. Chapter~7 therefore explores a scalable alternative: training on video--text pairs while \emph{simulating} reference conditioning through masking strategies and attention masks~\citep{zhou2025scaling}. This reframes identity-preserving control as a form of masked reconstruction, encouraging the model to learn stable identity features that persist over time.

Just as in PSG, \textbf{evaluation} is subtle. Traditional realism metrics (\eg, Fr\'echet Inception Distance (FID)~\citep{heusel2017gans} for images) do not measure whether the output satisfies constraints. In controllable generation, the most important failures are often local and semantic: a logo is distorted, a reference face changes slightly, or an edit bleeds into the background. For video, small per-frame deviations can accumulate into noticeable drift. Chapters~6--7 therefore emphasize controllability-focused evaluation, including identity preservation and temporal consistency, and use stress tests that amplify common failure modes (large pose changes, multi-reference inputs, longer temporal horizons). This evaluation perspective also motivates our focus on lightweight, training-time mechanisms. To be practical, controllability improvements should generalize across backbones and should not add heavy inference overhead.

\section{Overview of Contributions}
The results presented in this thesis have been published in five papers, organized as two parts: visual understanding and visual generation.
On the visual understanding side, these papers address long-tail bias, semantic ambiguity, and open-world generalization, and they treat relation prediction as the interface through which a scene can be controlled. On the visual generation side, they treat faithful control as an explicit learning objective, and focus on mechanisms that teach the model which information to preserve from a reference and where to apply it during denoising. In the terms of Section~\ref{sec:unifying_argument}, the first group widens what a user can control in a scene representation, and the second makes the control a user already has over a generated output actually effective. In both cases the gain comes from supervising the intended behavior directly rather than supplying an additional input.

\subsection{Panoptic Scene Graph Generation (Chapters~3--5)}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{HiLo (Chapter~3)}~\citep{zhou2023hilo} addresses long-tail PSG under semantic overlap. HiLo introduces frequency-specialized branches for high- and low-frequency predicates and enforces cross-branch consistency, improving long-tail relation recognition while preserving strong overall recall.
    \item \textbf{VLPrompt (Chapter~4)}~\citep{zhou2025vlprompt} augments PSG relation prediction with context-rich language cues elicited from large language models. A dual-attention prompter fuses object-pair visual features with complementary language signals, helping the model recover rare or ambiguous predicates.
    \item \textbf{OpenPSG (Chapter~5)}~\citep{zhou2025openpsg} extends PSG to open-world settings by pairing open-set panoptic segmentation with efficient pair filtering and autoregressive multimodal relation decoding. This enables grounded relation prediction beyond predefined vocabularies.
\end{itemize}
\noindent These three chapters can be read as a progression from \emph{robust closed-set prediction} to \emph{language-assisted relation reasoning} and finally to \emph{open-world grounded prediction}. Together, they establish PSG as a useful framework for improving long-tail robustness, language-assisted relation reasoning, and open-world scene understanding.

\subsection{Reference-Conditioned Image and Video Generation (Chapters~6--7)}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Leffa (Chapter~6)}~\citep{zhou2025learning} introduces an attention regularization loss for controllable person image generation. Instantiated through learnable flow fields in attention, it guides reference-conditioned diffusion models to focus on the correct source regions, reducing fine-grained detail distortion in tasks such as virtual try-on and pose transfer.
    \item \textbf{Saber (Chapter~7)}~\citep{zhou2025scaling} addresses reference-to-video generation with a scalable zero-shot training strategy that learns from ordinary video--text pairs. Through masked training, attention design, and mask augmentation, Saber preserves identity without requiring expensive reference image--video--text triplets.
\end{itemize}
\noindent Together, Chapters~6--7 show that faithful controllability depends on explicitly teaching the model what information should be preserved from the reference and how that information should be integrated during generation, both spatially and temporally.

Each of the five technical chapters is based on a paper on which I am the first author. The chapters differ in the size of the collaboration behind them, so this section states my individual contribution to each one. The same statement is repeated at the start of the corresponding chapter.

\paragraph{Chapters~3--5 (HiLo, VLPrompt, OpenPSG).}
These three chapters were completed during my doctoral research at King's College London with a small supervisory team. For all three, I identified the research problem, developed the method, implemented the models, designed and ran all experiments, carried out the analysis, and wrote the manuscripts. My co-authors provided supervisory guidance on research direction, on the formulation of the method, and on experimental design and writing. No part of the implementation or the experiments in these chapters was carried out by anyone else.

\paragraph{Chapter~6 (Leffa).}
This chapter is based on work developed during an internship at Meta, and the paper has fourteen authors. As first author, I proposed the core idea of the chapter. It interprets the cross-attention map between target queries and reference keys as a soft correspondence field, and regularizes that field during training by converting it into a flow field that warps the reference toward the target. I formulated the \leffa{} loss, implemented it, designed and ran all experiments and ablations reported in Chapter~6, carried out the analysis, and wrote the manuscript. My mentors, who are listed last among the authors, provided research guidance and feedback throughout. The remaining co-authors provided technical support, including engineering and infrastructure support for large-scale training, help with the human study, and comments on the manuscript. The long author list reflects the collaborative structure of an industrial research laboratory. The core technical contribution is my own.

\paragraph{Chapter~7 (Saber).}
This chapter is based on a second internship at Meta, and the paper has seventeen authors. As first author, I proposed the central idea: that reference-to-video generation can be learned from ordinary video--text pairs by building synthetic references from randomly sampled, partially masked frames, which removes the need for reference image--video--text triplets. I designed the masked training strategy, the attention-mask formulation, and the spatial mask augmentation used to suppress the copy-paste shortcut. I also implemented the method, designed and ran the OpenS2V-Eval experiments and ablations, and wrote the manuscript. As in Chapter~6, my mentors are listed last among the authors and provided research guidance, and the remaining co-authors provided technical support for training infrastructure and large-scale data pipelines, baseline reproduction, and evaluation. The core idea, the method design, the implementation, and the experimental work are my own.

\section{Thesis Organization}
Chapter~2 reviews related work on visual understanding and generation. It covers structured representations such as panoptic segmentation, scene graphs, and PSG; long-tail and open-world learning; vision--language prompting and large multimodal models; and diffusion-based conditioning and temporal consistency mechanisms for controllable video generation.
Chapters~3--5 present the visual understanding contributions and progressively broaden PSG capability from long-tail robustness (Chapter~3) to vision--language prompting (Chapter~4) and open-set relation prediction (Chapter~5).
Chapters~6--7 present the visual generation contributions, focusing on attention-based correspondence for reference-guided person image generation (Chapter~6) and scalable zero-shot reference-to-video generation trained without explicit triplet data (Chapter~7).
Chapter~8 concludes with a summary, limitations, and future research directions for both parts of the thesis.

Readers interested mainly in \emph{control over visual understanding} will find a unified view of PSG systems in Chapters~3--5. These chapters cover how to model relations under long-tail bias, how to add language priors without losing grounding, and how to move toward open-world settings in which the predicate vocabulary is not fixed. Readers interested mainly in \emph{reference-controlled generation} will find Chapters~6--7, which cover correspondence and identity preservation in diffusion models, from single-image person generation to scalable reference-to-video generation. Chapter~2 provides the algorithmic background for both sets of chapters. The two parts can be read in either order, but they are not independent. Section~\ref{sec:attention_soft_matching} develops the matching and alignment view that both parts use, and each part supplies half of the evidence for the claim in Section~\ref{sec:unifying_argument} that control must be learned rather than offered.
When possible, each chapter states its assumptions, problem setting, and evaluation protocols explicitly, so that readers can interpret the contributions even without deep familiarity with the surrounding literature.
