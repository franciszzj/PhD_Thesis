\chapter{Introduction}

\section{Motivation}
Human vision is not a passive classifier; it is a compositional reasoning engine.
When we look at a scene, we effortlessly identify objects, localize them, infer attributes, and understand relations—who holds what, what occludes what, and what part belongs to which instance.
Equally importantly, humans can imagine with intent: we can mentally render an object from a description, edit a scene while preserving identity, or generate plausible variations that satisfy constraints.

Recent advances in attention-based architectures, large-scale pretraining, and diffusion-based generative models have substantially improved recognition accuracy and image synthesis quality.
However, two persistent gaps remain.

First, structured visual understanding is still brittle in the wild.
Conventional recognition pipelines excel at category prediction but often underrepresent structure: relations are underconstrained, part–whole hierarchies are inconsistently captured, and long-tail concepts are poorly handled. These weaknesses become severe in open-world settings where the vocabulary expands beyond curated labels and where composition—rather than isolated classification—determines correctness.

Second, controllable visual generation remains unreliable under realistic user demands. While state-of-the-art generators can produce photorealistic images, they frequently misinterpret instructions, ignore fine-grained constraints, or entangle control signals with unintended changes. The central challenge is not merely to generate a plausible image, but to generate the image that satisfies explicit, multi-level controls (e.g., layout, attributes, relations, identity, style) while maintaining coherence and fidelity.

This thesis is driven by a simple premise: structure and control are two sides of the same coin. Structured representations provide the language of constraints; controllable generation tests whether those constraints are truly understood and operational.

\section{Thesis Scope and Problem Statement}
This thesis studies visual intelligence through two tightly connected directions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Structured Visual Understanding}: learning models that represent images as structured entities—instances, parts, attributes, and relations—and that remain effective under distribution shift, long-tail categories, and open-vocabulary settings.
    \item \textbf{Controllable Visual Generation}: designing generative systems that obey user-provided constraints with high precision, supporting controllable synthesis and editing while preserving realism and consistency.
\end{itemize}
The overarching goal is to advance both understanding and generation toward practical visual intelligence: systems that can reliably parse complex scenes and faithfully render user intent.

\section{Overview of Contributions}
The thesis comprises five papers, organized into two themes.

\subsection{Structured Visual Understanding (Chapters 3–5)}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Chapter 3: HiLo} (Paper 1) introduces a hierarchical modeling approach that captures both high-level semantics and low-level localization for panoptic scene graph generation.
    \item \textbf{Chapter 4: VLPrompt} (Paper 2) explores vision–language prompting as a mechanism for structured prediction, enabling knowledge transfer from pretrained models to improve generalization.
    \item \textbf{Chapter 5: OpenPSG} (Paper 3) advances panoptic scene graph generation to open-vocabulary and open-set conditions, addressing scalability and robustness in real-world scenarios.
\end{itemize}
Together, these chapters argue for explicit compositional structure and open-world readiness as first-class objectives in visual understanding.

\subsection{Controllable Visual Generation (Chapters 6–7)}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Chapter 6: Leffa} (Paper 4) investigates control interfaces and representations for faithful controllable image and video synthesis.
    \item \textbf{Chapter 7: Saber} (Paper 5) strengthens controllability under complex constraints with improved robustness and generalization.
\end{itemize}
Together, these chapters position controllable generation as an alignment problem: generation becomes dependable when the model’s generative factors correspond to human-interpretable constraints.

\section{Thesis Organization}
Chapter 2 reviews foundations in structured prediction, vision–language pretraining, scene graph reasoning, and controllable diffusion models.
Chapters 3–5 develop methods for structured visual understanding: HiLo, VLPrompt, and OpenPSG.
Chapters 6–7 present methods for controllable visual generation: Leffa and Saber.
Chapter 8 concludes with a unified discussion of structure–control duality and outlines future directions, including open-world structured generation and evaluation protocols for faithful controllability.
