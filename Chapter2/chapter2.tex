\chapter{Literature Review}

\section{Scope}
This chapter reviews research along two axes of visual intelligence that frame this thesis: structured visual understanding and controllable visual generation. We emphasize methods that model compositional structure at panoptic and relational levels, and generative approaches that provide reliable, fine-grained control for images and videos. Rather than enumerating an exhaustive bibliography, we organize the literature around recurring technical questions: (i) which \emph{representations} best capture structure; (ii) which \emph{learning strategies} address long-tail and open-world settings; and (iii) which \emph{control interfaces} enable faithful generation beyond text-only prompting.

\section{Structured Visual Understanding}
\subsection{Panoptic Segmentation and Structured Prediction}
Panoptic segmentation unifies instance- and semantic-level recognition by assigning every pixel to either a ``thing'' instance or a ``stuff'' region. Compared with bounding-box detection, panoptic masks offer stronger geometric grounding, which is valuable when downstream reasoning depends on spatial configuration, contact, occlusion, and fine boundaries. Modern approaches increasingly adopt transformer-based architectures and mask-centric decoders that jointly model global context and dense pixel assignments.

From a structured prediction standpoint, panoptic segmentation can be viewed as a foundation layer: it establishes entity hypotheses that can be enriched with attributes, parts, and interactions. Many structured tasks can be decomposed into (i) proposing a set of entities, (ii) learning contextualized representations for these entities, and (iii) predicting structured outputs (relations, roles, or hierarchies). The key difficulty is that errors are correlated: missing a small region can invalidate an interaction, and misclassifying a region can cascade to relation mistakes. These couplings motivate end-to-end representations that bind pixels, entities, and semantics.

\subsection{Scene Graph Generation}
Scene graph generation (SGG) represents a scene as entities connected by predicates. Early work adopted object-centric pipelines: detect entities, form subject--object pairs, and classify predicates with contextual features. Later approaches introduced message passing and graph neural networks to propagate context, and transformer-based models to capture higher-order interactions with global attention. In parallel, substantial work studied the role of biased datasets and evaluation protocols: relation distributions are highly skewed, and models can achieve deceptively strong scores by exploiting co-occurrence statistics.

Two problem families recur in SGG. \textbf{Context modeling} asks how to represent subject--object interactions beyond local appearance---including surrounding objects, global layout, and implicit functional cues. \textbf{Long-tail learning} asks how to avoid collapsing to frequent predicates such as \emph{on} or \emph{near}. Proposed remedies include re-sampling, reweighting, debiased losses, causal and counterfactual formulations, and calibration strategies. Finally, \textbf{weak supervision and transfer} have become increasingly important as label spaces grow; this motivates methods that reuse pretrained representations and minimize task-specific retraining.

\subsection{Panoptic Scene Graph Generation}
Panoptic scene graph generation (PSG) integrates panoptic segmentation with relational reasoning, producing scene graphs whose nodes are pixel-accurate entities. PSG raises modeling challenges: bridging coarse semantics and fine localization, aligning relation prediction with segmentation masks, and scaling to open-world vocabularies. This thesis advances PSG from three complementary angles: cross-granularity representation learning (HiLo), vision--language prompting for transferable structured prediction (VLPrompt), and open-vocabulary PSG with large multimodal models (OpenPSG).

Compared with classical SGG, PSG changes both the node space and the grounding. Nodes include ``stuff'' (e.g., \emph{road}, \emph{sky}) and dense instance masks, which often influence relations and provide contextual anchors. Pixel-level grounding offers richer geometry but also increases ambiguity: relations may depend on subtle cues such as contact boundaries, containment, or relative depth ordering. PSG therefore benefits from representations that fuse mask geometry, visual appearance, and global context.

PSG also stresses efficiency. Naively evaluating all entity pairs is quadratic in the number of segments, which can be expensive for panoptic predictions with many regions. Many approaches introduce filtering mechanisms, relation existence prediction, or sparse pairing strategies to reduce computation while preserving recall on informative pairs.

\subsection{Vision--Language Pretraining and Prompting}
Pretrained vision--language models provide rich alignment between visual content and natural language. Prompting---adapting a pretrained model via textual or multimodal instructions---has emerged as a data-efficient mechanism for transfer across tasks and label spaces. For structured prediction, prompting can supply commonsense priors, disambiguate long-tail relations, and provide flexible interfaces for evolving vocabularies without retraining heavy task heads.

Vision--language pretraining has changed the default assumption in recognition: rather than learning all semantics from scratch, models can inherit broad concept coverage from web-scale data. This is particularly relevant for relations, where lexical and commonsense knowledge (e.g., that \emph{person riding bicycle} is plausible) complements visual evidence. Prompting can be implemented in multiple ways, ranging from simple textual templates to learned prompt tokens and multimodal prompt modules that fuse language features with visual embeddings. Importantly, prompting provides an interface that remains stable when the label space changes, which is attractive for open-world structured understanding.

\subsection{Open-Vocabulary and Open-World Recognition}
Open-vocabulary recognition extends beyond fixed label sets by aligning visual features with language embeddings or by leveraging multimodal generative capacities. In segmentation, open-vocabulary models connect pixel-level features to text queries to recognize unseen categories. For relations, open-world settings require both discovering novel predicates and grounding them in visual evidence. These trends underpin our OpenPSG design, which combines open-set segmentation with open-set relational reasoning.

Open-vocabulary recognition comes with practical challenges. First, \textbf{linguistic ambiguity}: many visual concepts map to multiple surface forms, and predicates can be paraphrased in diverse ways. Second, \textbf{grounding}: a generated label must correspond to evidence in the image, not merely a plausible completion. Third, \textbf{calibration}: open-world models must balance abstention and prediction, especially when confronted with unseen categories. Recent work leverages large multimodal models as both generators and judges, combining free-form descriptions with verification steps to improve reliability. In PSG, these issues are amplified because errors in either segmentation or relation grounding can corrupt the structured output.

\section{Controllable Visual Generation}
\subsection{Diffusion Models and Conditioning Mechanisms}
Diffusion models have become the workhorse for high-fidelity image and video synthesis. Controllability is achieved through conditioning channels such as text prompts, layouts, edge maps, depth, segmentation, and reference features. Architectural and training strategies include encoder--decoder conditioning, cross-attention guidance, adapter and control branches, and lightweight fine-tuning modules that specialize controls while preserving base model quality.

At a high level, diffusion-based controllability can be understood as designing \emph{interfaces} that inject constraints into the denoising process. Cross-attention provides semantic alignment with text, while additional control branches can inject spatial and structural signals. Parameter-efficient strategies (e.g., adapters or low-rank updates) are widely used in practice because they reduce compute and preserve the base model's generality. A recurring challenge is \textbf{control--quality trade-off}: strong conditioning can overspecify the generation and harm realism, while weak conditioning yields realistic but unfaithful outputs. Effective control mechanisms therefore aim to modulate conditioning strength adaptively and to separate editable factors from invariant ones.

\subsection{Image Editing and Instruction Following}
Instruction-based editing aims to modify content while preserving identity and context. Successful methods combine localized guidance (e.g., masks, attention control, latent injection) with language-driven objectives to reduce over-editing and attribute leakage. Lightweight personalization techniques and parameter-efficient adapters further improve faithfulness to user intent under limited data.

Instruction following exposes failure modes that are rarely visible in unconditional generation. Models may over-edit backgrounds, change identity, or satisfy high-level semantics while violating fine-grained constraints. Localization mechanisms (explicit masks or implicit attention localization), identity-preserving representations, and iterative refinement strategies are common tools to mitigate these issues. In addition, multi-stage pipelines often separate \emph{what to change} (edit localization and semantics) from \emph{how to render} (photorealistic synthesis), which reduces unintended side effects.

\subsection{Layout, Geometry, and Identity Control}
Layout-to-image and geometry-guided generation provide explicit spatial structure that constrains object placement and interactions. Segmentation- or keypoint-based controls enable attribute and pose specification, while identity-preserving mechanisms maintain subject consistency across edits and compositions. These controls are particularly relevant to structured scenes where relations and part--whole organization matter.

Layout and geometry controls also serve as a bridge between understanding and generation. Structured outputs such as segmentation masks, depth maps, or scene graphs can be used as conditioning signals, turning a recognition representation into a generative interface. However, naively conditioning on structure can introduce rigidity: the model may satisfy spatial constraints but lose texture realism, or preserve texture while violating layout. Effective methods balance geometric fidelity and photorealism, often by fusing control signals at multiple resolutions and using learned representations rather than raw maps alone.

\subsection{Video Generation, Editing, and Temporal Consistency}
Extending control to video introduces temporal coherence and motion constraints. Methods align per-frame conditioning with temporal attention, optical flow, or latent propagation to mitigate flicker and drift. Instruction-following video editing further requires disentangling edit targets from background and maintaining long-range consistency across shots. Our controllable video efforts prioritize reliability under multi-condition inputs and realistic user constraints.

Temporal consistency is a central challenge because small per-frame deviations can accumulate into visible flicker. Approaches range from simple latent propagation to explicit temporal modules that model long-range dependencies. Conditioning is also more complex in video: text describes semantics, but motion and camera dynamics require additional signals. As a result, controllable video generation often combines multiple conditions (e.g., text with trajectory, layout, or reference frames), and success depends on how these signals are integrated and how conflicts are resolved.

\subsection{Evaluation Protocols}
Evaluating controllability demands metrics beyond perceptual quality. Typical measures include constraint satisfaction rates (for attributes, layout, and relations), identity preservation, and temporal stability in videos, alongside standard fidelity and diversity scores. Robust protocols stress-test compositional prompts and multi-signal conditioning to reveal failure modes.

For structured understanding, evaluation must disentangle segmentation quality from relation quality and must account for long-tailed predicates. Metrics such as mean recall emphasize rare classes and better reflect relational competence. For controllable generation, automatic metrics are often insufficient: human preference studies and task-specific evaluations (e.g., edit faithfulness, temporal stability, identity preservation) remain important. A practical benchmark suite should include compositional prompts, multi-constraint settings, and hard negatives that expose spurious correlations.

\section{Summary}
Across understanding and generation, a common theme emerges: explicit structure improves robustness and transfer, while dependable control turns generative models into practical visual interfaces. This thesis builds on these trends to advance PSG as a panoptic, relational, and increasingly open-world understanding task, and to advance controllable image and video generation toward faithful constraint satisfaction under realistic user demands.
