\section{Related Work}
\label{openpsg:sec:related_work}

\subsection{Panoptic Scene Graph Generation}
Panoptic scene graph generation stems from scene graph generation (SGG) by replacing bounding boxes with panoptic segmentation masks to represent the objects, so as to achieve a more comprehensive understanding of scenes.
Following the introduction of PSG~\citep{yang2022panoptic}, a series of related works~\citep{yang2022panoptic, wang2023pair, zhou2023hilo, li2023panoptic, zhao2023textpsg, zhou2025vlprompt} emerge, significantly advancing its performance.
For example, \citet{yang2022panoptic}, based on DETR~\citep{carion2020end}, introduce an end-to-end framework with learnable queries to generate panoptic scene graphs.
\citet{wang2023pair} design a pair proposal network to filter irrelative subject-object pairs, achieving performance improvement of the PSG.
Subsequently, \citet{zhou2023hilo} build a HiLo architecture based on Mask2Former~\citep{cheng2022masked} and devise separate branches for high- and low-frequency relations respectively, hence achieving an unbiased relation prediction method.
\citet{li2023panoptic} re-balance the relation prediction  by adaptively transferring information from high-frequency to low-frequency relations.
Additionally, \citet{zhao2023textpsg} implement a weakly-supervised PSG method given only image-text pairs as annotations, allowing for the learning of panoptic scene graphs from image-level ground truth.
Recently, \citet{zhou2025vlprompt} leverage the rich language information inherent in large language models~\citep{achiam2023gpt} and design effective image-text interaction modules to assist unbiased PSG.
All these works are learned in the closed-set setting, in this chapter, we study the open-set PSG, which has been unexplored.

\subsection{Open-Set Scene Graph Generation}
Open-set SGG has been studied in recent years. 
Earlier works~\citep{kan2021zero, yu2022zero}, often termed as zero-shot SGG, focus on transferring the knowledge from known relations to unknown relations given their prior connections; for example, \citet{kan2021zero} leverage the external commonsense knowledge while \citet{yu2022zero} use knowledge graphs for the knowledge transfer.
Subsequently, with the advancement of large multimodal models, various open-set SGG works~\citep{he2022towards, zhang2023learning, yu2023visually, chen2023expanding} have emerged; for example, \citet{he2022towards} and \citet{zhang2023learning} focus on predicting relations between unknown objects in the SGG using multimodal models.
\citet{yu2023visually} and \citet{chen2023expanding} on the other hand focus on the open-set relation prediction in PSG, sharing the same aim with us.
Specifically, \citet{yu2023visually} utilize the CLIP model to match visual features with textual relation features for open-set relation prediction; \citet{chen2023expanding} employ a student-teacher network to align visual concepts in multimodal models for predicting open-set relations.
In this chapter, different from previous works, we introduce an auto-regressive method based on the LMM to achieve open-set relation prediction.

\subsection{Large Multimodal Models}
Since the introduction of large models like the GPT series~\citep{achiam2023gpt}, recent years have witnessed rapid development in large multimodal models~\citep{radford2021learning, li2023blip, liu2024visual}.
Benefiting from its nature of connecting vision and language, LMMs have significantly advanced various downstream tasks, ranging from computer vision~\citep{du2022learning, yu2023towards} to natural language processing~\citep{wang2020language, joshi2019bert, raffel2020exploring}. 
Early multimodal models, such as CLIP~\citep{radford2021learning}, trained on image-text paired datasets through contrastive learning to align the visual and textual information.
Subsequently, owing to enlightenment of the autoregressive prediction in large language models~\citep{achiam2023gpt, touvron2023llama}, there has been an explosive growth of LMMs~\citep{li2023blip, liu2023improved, liu2024visual}; by introducing mechanisms that can transform visual information into large language models, they facilitate the communication between visual and textual information.
Furthermore, this has endowed LMMs with the capability to generate free text, leading to substantial improvements in numerous multimodal tasks.
In this chapter, we leverage LMMs to design a multimodal relation decoder to predict relations in an open-set scenarios.


\subsection{Developments Since Publication}
\label{openpsg:sec:post_publication}
OpenPSG appeared at ECCV 2024. Open-vocabulary relation prediction has become an active area since then, and later work bears on this chapter in two ways. It has refined the modeling, and, more importantly for the discussion in Sec.~\ref{openpsg:sec:limitations}, it has begun to address the evaluation problem that this chapter identifies but does not solve.

\noindent \textbf{Modeling.}
Several lines of work develop the prompting interface that OpenPSG uses only in a basic form. \citet{liu2025relation} build hierarchical, region-aware prompts and add a dynamic selection mechanism that chooses which text prompts are relevant to the visual content, which reduces the noise created when a fixed instruction is applied to every pair. INOVA~\citep{li2025inova} targets a different weakness. It introduces interaction-aware pre-training that explicitly separates interacting from non-interacting object pairs, together with interaction-guided query selection. This directly improves on our relation existence estimation query, which is a learned binary filter with a fixed threshold and no notion of what makes a pair interaction-like. Since that filter is our only defense against a decoder that never abstains, this is a substantial advance rather than an incremental one. A parallel line replaces the frozen decoder entirely with MLLMs trained end-to-end for graph generation~\citep{feng2026sggr3, makarov2026scenegraphvlm}, which removes the ceiling that our frozen BLIP-2 imposes on the achievable relation vocabulary.

\noindent \textbf{Evaluation.}
The more significant development concerns measurement. Section~\ref{openpsg:sec:limitations} argues that the base/novel protocol used here measures held-out-vocabulary generalization rather than open-set prediction, and that exact-match scoring penalizes the paraphrases a generative decoder naturally produces. Later work takes up both points. \citet{neau2025measuring} argue that existing SGG benchmarks have vocabularies that are too limited to assess open-vocabulary capability at all, and develop reference-free evaluation that scores image-relation alignment directly instead of scoring against a fixed annotation. This is exactly the property our tables cannot measure. Other work approaches the same problem through predicate synonym sets, which admit semantic equivalence into the matching criterion. On the hallucination side, the reward used by SceneGraphVLM~\citep{makarov2026scenegraphvlm} explicitly penalizes unsupported relations, and therefore treats ungroundedness as a quantity to be measured rather than a risk to be acknowledged.

\noindent \textbf{Assessment.}
The architectural contributions of this chapter remain sound: filtering pairs before expensive decoding, and generating relations autoregressively from pair features. The efficiency argument for filtering becomes stronger as decoders grow larger. The frozen-component design is the part most clearly superseded. The evaluation critique, which this chapter could only state, is now being addressed with concrete instruments. A researcher building on OpenPSG today should adopt reference-free or synonym-aware evaluation instead of the base/novel split used here, and we regard this as the single most useful change to the protocol.
