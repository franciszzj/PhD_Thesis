\begin{abstract}
This thesis studies two major capabilities of visual intelligence: \emph{structured understanding}, which converts pixels into organized representations that support downstream reasoning, and \emph{controllable generation}, which synthesizes visual content that remains faithful to user intent. Despite rapid progress driven by large-scale pretraining and diffusion models, current systems still face two central limitations. On the understanding side, they often recognize objects without capturing precise entity relations under long-tailed and open-world settings. On the generation side, they produce realistic images and videos but still struggle to follow fine-grained constraints over appearance, identity, and motion. This thesis addresses these two challenges through parallel investigations of structured understanding and controllable generation.

For structured understanding, this thesis focuses on panoptic scene graph generation (PSG), which combines panoptic segmentation with relation prediction to build pixel-grounded scene graphs over both ``things'' and ``stuff''. PSG provides a natural testbed for compositional reasoning because it requires accurate localization, interaction modeling, and semantic generalization at the same time. Chapters~3--5 advance PSG from three complementary perspectives. \emph{HiLo} improves relation recognition under severe long-tailed distributions by explicitly separating high- and low-frequency predicates while enforcing consistency between them, leading to stronger recovery of informative rare relations. \emph{VLPrompt} introduces language as external commonsense supervision, using vision--language prompting to supply context-rich relational cues that complement visual evidence and improve recognition and generalization when predicate spaces evolve. \emph{OpenPSG} further extends PSG beyond closed vocabularies by combining open-set panoptic perception with relation querying and multimodal generation, enabling grounded relation prediction in open-world settings. Together, these studies show that PSG can serve not only as a richer representation of scenes but also as a principled framework for improving long-tail robustness, multimodal reasoning, and open-world scene understanding.

For controllable generation, this thesis studies how image and video synthesis can better preserve user-specified reference while maintaining fidelity and consistency. Chapters~6--7 address this problem in two representative settings. \emph{Leffa} tackles controllable person image generation, such as virtual try-on and pose transfer, by identifying misaligned cross-reference attention as a major source of detail distortion and introducing an attention regularization strategy that improves correspondence between reference appearance and target content without adding inference cost. \emph{Saber} addresses controllable reference-to-video generation, where a model must follow textual instructions while preserving subject identity over time. To avoid dependence on expensive reference--video--text triplets, it proposes a zero-shot training paradigm that simulates reference conditioning from ordinary video--text data, allowing scalable training while improving identity preservation and temporal coherence. Taken together, these works show that controllability in generation depends on making constraints explicit in the learning and attention mechanisms rather than relying on realism alone.

Overall, this thesis argues that progress in visual intelligence requires both richer structure for understanding and stronger control for generation. By advancing PSG as a pixel-grounded relational representation and by developing more reliable mechanisms for controllable image and video synthesis, the thesis moves toward visual systems that are not only more accurate and expressive, but also more useful as practical interfaces for reasoning and creation.
\end{abstract}
