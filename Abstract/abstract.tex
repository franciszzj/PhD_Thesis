\begin{abstract}
This thesis studies two major dimensions of visual intelligence: visual understanding and visual generation, both of which have advanced rapidly in recent years. However, important limitations remain in each direction. On the understanding side, current systems often recognize objects without producing \emph{structured understanding} of entities and their relations, especially under long-tailed and open-world settings. On the generation side, current systems can synthesize realistic images and videos but still struggle with \emph{controllable generation} under fine-grained constraints over appearance, identity, and motion. This thesis focuses on these two research problems: structured understanding and controllable generation.

For the structured understanding direction, the thesis focuses on panoptic scene graph generation (PSG), a task that segments an image into panoptic entities and predicts subject--object--relation triplets to form a pixel-grounded scene graph over both ``things'' and ``stuff'' categories. PSG requires accurate localization, interaction modeling, and semantic generalization, making it a natural testbed for compositional reasoning. Chapters~3--5 address three key challenges in this setting. \emph{HiLo} introduces frequency-specialized branches for high- and low-frequency predicates and enforces cross-branch consistency, improving long-tail relation recognition under semantic overlap. \emph{VLPrompt} augments relation prediction with context-rich language cues elicited from large language models (LLMs), helping the model recover rare or ambiguous predicates. \emph{OpenPSG} extends PSG to open-world settings by pairing open-set panoptic segmentation with efficient pair filtering and autoregressive multimodal relation decoding, enabling grounded prediction beyond predefined relation vocabularies. Together, these studies establish PSG as a useful framework for improving long-tail robustness, language-assisted relation reasoning, and open-world scene understanding.

For the controllable generation direction, the thesis studies how image and video synthesis can better preserve user-specified reference while maintaining fidelity and consistency. Chapters~6--7 address this problem in two representative settings. \emph{Leffa} introduces an attention regularization loss for controllable person image generation that guides reference-conditioned diffusion models to focus on the correct source regions, reducing detail distortion in tasks such as virtual try-on and pose transfer. \emph{Saber} addresses reference-to-video generation with a zero-shot training strategy that learns from ordinary video--text pairs, using masked training and attention design to preserve identity without requiring expensive reference image--video--text triplets. Taken together, these works show that faithful controllability depends on explicitly teaching the model, through both training signals and attention design, what information should be preserved from the reference and how it should be integrated during generation.

Overall, the thesis advances visual intelligence by addressing unresolved problems in these two directions: richer pixel-grounded relational representations for structured understanding, and more reliable control mechanisms for image and video generation. These contributions move toward visual systems that are not only more accurate and expressive, but also more useful as practical interfaces for reasoning and creation.
\end{abstract}
